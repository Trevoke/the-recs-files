#+TITLE: Nygard Architecture Decision Records — Reference
#+STARTUP: overview

Not a project document. This records what the source material actually says, so
that when the project borrows a form or a term we can check the borrowing.
Paraphrased from the sources listed at the end, with the load-bearing sentences quoted.

*What it establishes.* Michael Nygard's 2011 blog post proposed the
Architecture Decision Record (ADR): a short, numbered text file, kept in the
project's repository, that records one architecturally significant decision
under five headings — Title, Context, Decision, Status, Consequences. A record
is never deleted; a decision that is reversed is marked superseded and points
at the record that replaced it. The form has since been catalogued and
extended (MADR, Y-statements) and sits beside an older, heavier template
(Tyree and Akerman, 2005). This entry gives what Nygard wrote, how the
variants differ, and what practitioners report goes wrong, so a record
written "in Nygard format" can be checked against the original.

* An ADR exists because the motivation behind a decision is lost
Nygard opens on the problem of lost rationale:

#+begin_quote
One of the hardest things to track during the life of a project is the
motivation behind certain decisions.
#+end_quote

A newcomer who meets a decision without its reason has, in his telling, two
options, both bad: blindly accept it, or blindly change it. Accepting it may
keep a choice whose reasons no longer hold; changing it may break something
the original decision was protecting. He says it is better to avoid either.

* Large documents fail; small records are read
Nygard rejects the large architecture document as the remedy:

#+begin_quote
Large documents are never kept up to date.
#+end_quote

and argues for small, modular records instead:

#+begin_quote
Bite sized pieces are easier for for all stakeholders to consume.
#+end_quote

(The doubled "for" is in the original.) He also says, in paraphrase, that
nobody reads large documents either, and that agile methods are opposed not
to documentation but only to documentation without value.

* An ADR records one architecturally significant decision
The post is itself written as a decision, and its central sentence is:

#+begin_quote
We will keep a collection of records for "architecturally significant"
decisions: those that affect the structure, non-functional characteristics,
dependencies, interfaces, or construction techniques.
#+end_quote

So the test for significance is effect, not size: a decision belongs in an
ADR when it shapes structure, a quality attribute, a dependency, an interface,
or the way the system is built. adr.github.io states the same idea in its own
vocabulary: an architectural decision is a justified design choice that
addresses an architecturally significant requirement, one with a measurable
effect on the architecture and quality of the system (paraphrased). Each ADR
records one such decision; the set of them is the project's /decision log/.

Nygard's examples of what an ADR is about are technology and pattern
choices — his sample titles are "ADR 1: Deployment on Ruby on Rails 3.0.10"
and "ADR 9: LDAP for Multitenant Integration".

* The format is five sections: Title, Context, Decision, Status, Consequences
** Title names the decision as a short noun phrase
Each record has a short noun-phrase name, prefixed by its number, as in the
two examples above. The title names the decision, not the problem.

** Context states the forces, neutrally
The Context section describes the forces at play:

#+begin_quote
This section describes the forces at play, including technological,
political, social, and project local.
#+end_quote

Nygard notes that these forces are probably in tension and should be called
out as such, and that:

#+begin_quote
The language in this section is value-neutral.
#+end_quote

It states facts. It does not argue for the outcome; the argument belongs in
the Decision.

** Decision states the response, in active voice
#+begin_quote
This section describes our response to these forces. It is stated in full
sentences, with active voice.
#+end_quote

The convention, shown in the post's own text, is that the decision begins
"We will …". The post's own decisions read "We will keep a collection of
records…", "We will keep ADRs in the project repository…", "We will write
each ADR as if it is a conversation with a future developer."

** Status says where the decision stands
#+begin_quote
A decision may be "proposed" if the project stakeholders haven't agreed with
it yet, or "accepted" once it is agreed.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
If a later ADR changes or reverses a decision, it may be marked as
"deprecated" or "superseded" with a reference to its replacement.
#+end_quote

** Consequences records everything that follows, good and bad
#+begin_quote
This section describes the resulting context, after applying the decision.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
All consequences should be listed here, not just the "positive" ones.
#+end_quote

Nygard adds, in paraphrase, that a decision may have positive, negative and
neutral consequences, and that all of them affect the team and the project
in the future. The Consequences of one record are therefore likely to become
the Context of a later one.

* A record is one or two pages, written for a future developer
#+begin_quote
The whole document should be one or two pages long.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
We will write each ADR as if it is a conversation with a future developer.
#+end_quote

He asks for full sentences organised into paragraphs; bullets are acceptable
for visual style, not as an excuse for writing sentence fragments
(paraphrased).

* Records are numbered once, and the number is never reused
#+begin_quote
ADRs will be numbered sequentially and monotonically. Numbers will not be
reused.
#+end_quote

The number is the record's identity. Because a superseded record is kept, its
number stays taken.

* Records live in the repository, beside the code
#+begin_quote
We will keep ADRs in the project repository under doc/arch/adr-NNN.md
#+end_quote

He suggests a lightweight text format such as Markdown or Textile. The
ThoughtWorks Technology Radar, which rated "Lightweight Architecture Decision
Records" Trial in November 2016 and Adopt from November 2017, gives the
reason:

#+begin_quote
We recommend storing these details in source control, instead of a wiki or
website, as then they can provide a record that remains in sync with the code
itself.
#+end_quote

Tools vary the path: adr-tools defaults to =doc/adr= with files named
=NNNN-slug.md= (four digits, zero-padded); MADR defaults to =docs/decisions/=
with the same naming.

* A superseded record stays, and points at its successor
#+begin_quote
If a decision is reversed, we will keep the old one around, but mark it as
superseded.
#+end_quote

Nygard's reason, in paraphrase: it is still relevant to know that it /was/
the decision, even after it stopped being so. Superseding is therefore a new
record, not an edit: the old record's Status changes to superseded with a
reference to the new one, and its other sections stand as written.

Nat Pryce's adr-tools makes the link two-way. =adr new -s 9 <title>= creates
the new record with a "Supersedes" link to record 9, and rewrites record 9's
Status to "Superseded by" the new one (the tool's own text spells it
"Superceded"). =adr new -l "5:Amends:Amended by" <title>= creates any other
two-way relation the same way. =adr init= creates the log with record 1,
"Record architecture decisions", which itself cites Nygard's post.

Martin Fowler's bliki entry (March 2026) states the rule that follows from
this, as a practice rather than Nygard's own wording:

#+begin_quote
Once an ADR is accepted, it should never be reopened or changed - instead it
should be superseded.
#+end_quote

Nygard names /deprecated/ and /superseded/ side by side and defines neither
apart from the other; the post gives no rule for choosing between them. In
common use, superseded means another record replaced this one, and
deprecated means the decision was withdrawn — that reading is practice, not
Nygard's text.

* The variants differ in how much reasoning they make explicit
| Form | Origin | Sections | What it adds to Nygard |
|------+--------+----------+------------------------|
| Nygard | Cognitect blog, 2011 | Title, Context, Decision, Status, Consequences | — |
| MADR 4.0.0 | adr.github.io, 2024 | Title; Context and Problem Statement; Considered Options; Decision Outcome (mandatory). Status, date, decision-makers, consulted, informed; Decision Drivers; Consequences; Confirmation; Pros and Cons of the Options; More Information (optional) | Explicit options with pros and cons; drivers; how compliance is confirmed |
| Y-statement | Zimmermann, SATURN 2012 | One sentence: context, facing, decided for, neglected, to achieve, accepting that (optionally because) | Fits a decision in one sentence; names rejected options and accepted downside |
| Tyree/Akerman | IEEE Software 22(2), 2005 | Issue, Decision, Status, Group, Assumptions, Constraints, Positions, Argument, Implications, Related decisions, Related requirements, Related artifacts, Related principles, Notes | Assumptions, constraints, considered positions and traceability; much heavier |

Notes on each:

- *MADR* stands for "Markdown Architectural Decision Records" in its current
  version, having briefly stood for "Markdown Any Decision Records". Its
  Context and Problem Statement is described as two or three sentences or an
  illustrative story (paraphrased). The main difference from Nygard is that
  the options considered are a required section, where Nygard leaves them to
  the Context if at all.
- *Y-statement.* The template, as adr.github.io gives it, reads: "In the
  context of <use case/user story>, facing <concern>, we decided for <option>
  and neglected <other options>, to achieve <system qualities>, accepting
  <downside>, because <rationale>." Zimmermann's worked example decides for
  the Database Session State pattern, neglecting client and server session
  state, to achieve cloud elasticity, accepting that a session database must
  be designed and replicated. The "because" clause is a later extension; the
  original six parts end at "accepting that".
- *Tyree and Akerman* predate Nygard and come from enterprise architecture.
  Their Issue field is to leave no doubt why the issue is being addressed
  now; Positions lists the viable alternatives; Argument says why one was
  chosen; Implications corresponds to Nygard's Consequences (all
  paraphrased). Field list taken from the template as reproduced in Joel
  Parker Henderson's ADR collection, not from the paper itself.
- *ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010* also specifies information items for a decision
  record in an annex; adr.github.io lists it, and it is not covered here.

* Practitioners report the same handful of failures
Collected from Zimmermann's catalogue of ADR anti-patterns (2023), Fowler's
bliki entry, and the adr.github.io material. Names are Zimmermann's.

- *One-sided consequences* ("Fairy Tale", "Free Lunch Coupon") — only the
  benefits are listed, or the hard and long-term costs are left out. This is
  the failure Nygard's "not just the positive ones" sentence forbids.
- *Advocacy in place of context* ("Sales Pitch") — marketing language and
  unsupported claims. It breaks the value-neutral Context.
- *Straw-man or single option* ("Dummy Alternative", "Sprint/Rush") — an
  unworkable alternative set up to lose, or only one option considered with
  only short-term effects.
- *Narrow view* ("Tunnel Vision") — the decision considered in one local
  context, ignoring wider system effects or other stakeholders.
- *Off-topic content* ("Maze") — the title promises one decision and the
  text discusses something else.
- *A policy dressed as a decision* ("Blueprint/Policy in Disguise") — the
  record reads as a prescriptive cookbook rather than a report of a choice
  and its reasons.
- *Overgrowth* ("Mega-ADR", "Novel/Epic") — diagrams, specifications and
  narrative piled into the record until it becomes the large document Nygard
  argued against. Fowler's advice is to link supporting material rather
  than include it.
- *Editing an accepted record* — rewriting history rather than superseding;
  the reason the old decision held is lost, which is the problem ADRs exist
  to solve (Fowler, Nygard).
- *Recording too much or too little* — Zimmermann advises choosing by
  significance and priority, and not deferring decisions that are costly to
  undo; he also recommends stating one's confidence in the decision.

* A worked example
/Own illustration, not from any source./ A record in Nygard's form:

#+begin_example
ADR 7: Message queue for job notifications

Status: Accepted. Supersedes ADR 3.

Context
Jobs finish on worker hosts and the web front end must learn of it.
ADR 3 chose polling the jobs table every five seconds. At the current
volume of 40,000 jobs a day, polling accounts for a third of database
load. The team already operates a RabbitMQ cluster for billing. Two
consumers besides the front end, the audit log and the mailer, now
also want completion events.

Decision
We will publish a message to a "job.finished" topic when a job
completes, and the front end, the audit log and the mailer will
subscribe to it. We will stop polling the jobs table.

Consequences
Database load from the front end falls to near zero for this path.
New consumers subscribe without changes to the worker. Delivery now
depends on the RabbitMQ cluster: when it is down, completions are not
seen until it recovers, so workers must retry publishing. A message
delivered twice must be harmless, so every consumer must be idempotent.
The team takes on monitoring of queue depth.
#+end_example

ADR 3's Status would change to "Superseded by ADR 7", with its text
otherwise untouched.
