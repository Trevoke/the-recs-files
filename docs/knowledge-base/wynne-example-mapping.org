#+TITLE: Wynne's Example Mapping — Reference
#+STARTUP: overview

Not a project document. This records what the source material actually says, so
that when the project borrows a form or a term we can check the borrowing.
Paraphrased from the sources listed at the end, with the load-bearing sentences quoted.

*What it establishes.* Matt Wynne's Example Mapping is a way of running the
conversation that settles what a piece of work must do, before anyone builds
it. Four kinds of card are laid on a table as people talk: the story
(yellow), the rules it must keep (blue), the examples that illustrate each
rule (green), and the questions nobody present can answer (red). The shape
of the map shows how well the work is understood. Its examples are rough and
named in a phrase, not written as Gherkin. Cucumber later added a Rule
keyword to Gherkin so that a written specification could keep the same
rule-and-example structure.

*On the sources.* Wynne's own introduction (2015), cited [Wynne], is the
primary source and was read in full. Seb Rose's 2021 post on the Rule
keyword, cited [Rose], is by Wynne's colleague and co-author, and is the
source for what survives the map into Gherkin. The Gherkin changelog, cited
[Gherkin CHANGELOG], records the keyword's arrival. /The BDD Books:
Discovery/ (Nagy and Rose), which treats Example Mapping at length, was not
read.

* Example Mapping structures the conversation that settles a story

#+begin_quote
Before you pull a user story into development, it's crucial to have a
conversation to clarify and confirm the acceptance criteria. [Wynne]
#+end_quote

Teams call it backlog refinement, a three amigos meeting, a specification
workshop or a discovery workshop. Wynne's diagnosis of why it fails:

#+begin_quote
Whatever you call this conversation, many teams find it hard; it's
unstructured, it takes too long and gets boring. The result is they don't do
it regularly or consistently, or maybe they just give up on it entirely.
[Wynne]
#+end_quote

Its aim is shared understanding, not a document:

#+begin_quote
I think the true purpose, however, is to reach a shared understanding of what
it will take for the story to be done. You can move much more quickly towards
this goal by staying low-tech. [Wynne]
#+end_quote

* Four cards: story, rule, example, question

Examples come first, and the other kinds of information come out of
discussing them [Wynne]:

#+begin_quote
Concrete examples are a tremendous way to help us explore the problem domain,
and they make a great basis for our acceptance tests. But as we discuss these
examples, there are other things coming out in the conversation that deserve
to be captured too: rules that summarise a bunch of examples, or express other
agreed constraints about the scope of the story. questions about scenarios
where nobody in the conversations knows what the right outcome is. Or
assumptions we're making in order to progress. new user stories we discovered
or sliced and deferred out of scope. [Wynne]
#+end_quote

| Card     | Colour | Placed                              | Holds                                         |
|----------+--------+-------------------------------------+-----------------------------------------------|
| Story    | yellow | top of the table                    | the story under discussion                    |
| Rule     | blue   | in a row beneath the story          | "each of the acceptance criteria, or rules that we already know" |
| Example  | green  | in a column beneath its rule        | an example that illustrates that rule         |
| Question | red    | wherever it came up                 | what "nobody in the room can answer"          |

The layout and the quoted cells are from [Wynne]'s "How it works". "We keep
going until the group is satisfied that the scope of the story is clear, or
we run out of time" [Wynne]. Assumptions are named as something to capture
but given no colour; a newly found or sliced-off story takes another yellow
card [Wynne].

* A rule summarises examples, and some rules need none

A rule is either a summary of examples or an agreed constraint on scope
[Wynne, quoted above]. Examples exist to remove a rule's ambiguity; when the
rule is clear without them, none are written:

#+begin_quote
You'll find that some rules are so obvious you don't need examples at all.
It's clear from the conversation that everyone understands the rule. Great!
You can all move on with your lives without forcing yourselves to grind out
examples like BDD-brainwashed automatons. [Wynne]
#+end_quote

Rose states the dependence both ways, and the one-rule limit:

#+begin_quote
Each example in an example map illustrates one (and only one) rule. The
relationship between rule and example is critical. Without its examples, a
rule may be ambiguous. Without a rule, an example lacks context. Together they
fully specify an expected behaviour of the product and guide the development
team's efforts. [Rose]
#+end_quote

Wynne says the distinction between rules and examples must be "clearly
understood" before the technique can be used [Wynne].

* An example without a known outcome is a question

#+begin_quote
When the outcome (Then) is unclear, you don't have an example, you have a
question. [Wynne]
#+end_quote

When the conversation circles, it is short of information, not of opinion:

#+begin_quote
Instead of letting everyone share their opinion about what they think the
outcome should be, simply capture the question and move on. Congratulations!
You've just turned an unknown unknown into a known unknown. [Wynne]
#+end_quote

The remedy for a question is outside the room: someone missing from the
conversation, user research, or a spike [Wynne].

* The shape of the map tells the group what it knows

#+begin_quote
A table covered in red (question) cards tells us that we still have a lot to
learn about this story.
A table covered in blue (rule) cards tells us that this story is big and
complicated. Perhaps we can slice it? Take another yellow (story) card and put
the sliced story on the backlog.
A rule with many examples might be over-complex. Are there multiple rules at
play that need to be teased apart? [Wynne]
#+end_quote

- *Rules are where a story splits*: "You'll find that rules make natural
  fault lines for slicing your story" [Wynne].
- *A time-box*: "A small group of amigos should be able to map out a
  well-understood, well-sized story in about 25 minutes" [Wynne]. Overrunning
  means the team is still learning the technique, the story is too big, or it
  is still too uncertain. Readiness is decided by a thumb-vote, and remaining
  questions may be judged minor enough to resolve along the way [Wynne].
- *Who attends*: at minimum "a developer, a tester and a product person", plus
  anyone "likely to help you discover new questions, or to turn questions into
  decisions" [Wynne].
- *How often*: one story at a time, "every other day is often a good rhythm"
  [Wynne].

* Examples are rough phrases, not Gherkin

#+begin_quote
Many teams assume that the three amigos should write acceptance tests (e.g.
Cucumber scenarios) during this session, sitting around a projector while
someone types formal gherkin scenarios into an IDE. There are occasions when
this is valuable, but generally I think it's a bad idea. It can actually be
distracting from the true purpose of the conversation. [Wynne]
#+end_quote

Instead, each example is named in the manner of a /Friends/ episode title:

#+begin_example
The one where the customer forgot his receipt
The one where the product is damaged
The one where the product was bought 15 days ago
#+end_example

"You still don't need to resort to the rigid structure of Given When Then
just yet" [Wynne]. Writing Gherkin is a separate step that needs "a different
mode of thinking from deciding which examples are in scope, and clarifying
the underlying rules" [Wynne]. On a team with a mature domain language, the
developer and tester draft it and the product person reviews it, asking "Is
that how I would have written it?", which tests whether the session passed
on what the product person knew [Wynne]. Early in a project, writing Gherkin
together is where the ubiquitous language develops [Wynne].

Nothing requires cards: distributed teams use "bullet lists in a shared
Google doc", a spreadsheet with coloured cells, or a mind-map [Wynne].

* Only rules and examples outlive the map

Rose calls the step from map to written specification /formulation/, and
says which cards survive it:

#+begin_quote
Of the four card types used in example mapping, only two of them should be
captured in feature files – the rules and the examples. [Rose]
#+end_quote

- *The story* does not, "because a user story is a transient artefact of
  prioritisation and planning" [Rose].
- *The questions* do not, "because they should be answered before the example
  map is formulated as scenarios. The answers to the question cards may well
  have caused the creation of rules and/or examples which need to be
  documented in the feature file, but the questions themselves are transient"
  [Rose].
- *Each example* becomes one scenario: "Each scenario in a feature file
  represents a single example card from the example map" [Rose].

* Gherkin added a Rule keyword to match the map

Example Mapping came after Gherkin, and Gherkin had no place for the rule an
example illustrates. "The root cause is that Matt discovered example mapping
/after/ BDD, Cucumber, and Gherkin were created" [Rose]. For over five years
rules were listed in a feature's free-text description, with no way to tie a
scenario to its rule [Rose].

Gherkin 6.0.13 (2018-09-25) added the keyword:

#+begin_quote
This major release aligns Gherkin with Example Mapping, a collaborative
technique for /designing/ scenarios and discovering details about rules and
behaviour. A new `Rule` keyword has been introduced, and acts as a grouping
of one or more `Example`s - a new synonym for `Scenario`. ... We hope they
will guide people towards thinking of scenarios as /examples of business
rules/ rather than a series of form submissions and link clicking.
[Gherkin CHANGELOG]
#+end_quote

Rose's example of the result [Rose]:

#+begin_example
Feature: Library changes
  Rule: Members pay reservation of $1 per item
    Scenario: Reserving a single book
      Given Andrew is a member
      When he reserves one book
      Then he is charged $1.00
#+end_example

Tags on a Rule, inherited by its scenarios, followed in Gherkin 18.0.0
(2021-03-24) [Gherkin CHANGELOG; Rose].

Points the example is built to show: each green card illustrates exactly one
blue card; the examples are phrases with no Given/When/Then; "the borrower
reserved it themselves" is an example whose outcome is known (it surfaced
because the rule's wording was loose), while the overdue case has no known
outcome and so is a red card, not a green one; the story card names the
work and would not survive formulation.
