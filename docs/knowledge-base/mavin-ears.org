#+TITLE: Mavin's EARS — Reference
#+STARTUP: overview

Not a project document. This records what the source material actually says, so
that when the project borrows a form or a term we can check the borrowing.
Paraphrased from the sources listed at the end, with the load-bearing sentences quoted.

*What it establishes.* Alistair Mavin's Easy Approach to Requirements Syntax
(EARS) is a way of writing a requirement as one English sentence whose
clauses always come in the same order: an optional state, an optional
trigger, the name of the system, and "shall" followed by what the system
does. Which clauses a sentence uses puts it in one of five patterns
(ubiquitous, state driven, event driven, optional feature, unwanted
behaviour), and sentences that use more than one keyword are called complex.
It was built to keep requirements in natural language, so that they need no
translation into another notation and no specialist tool.

*On the sources.* The four papers by the originators (RE'09, RE'10, RE'16,
/IEEE Software/ 2019) are behind the IEEE paywall and were not read. The 2009
abstract is quoted from the University of Manchester's repository and the
2019 abstract from Semantic Scholar's record of it. The patterns, the
ruleset and the examples are quoted from Mavin's own page, cited [Mavin],
which is the originator's current statement of the notation. Wikipedia,
cited [Wikipedia], is used only for the bibliography and for the limitations,
which no primary source read here states; treat those as secondary. Kiro's
documentation, cited [Kiro], is quoted to show one current adoption.

* EARS gently constrains natural language, rather than replacing it

#+begin_quote
The Easy Approach to Requirements Syntax (EARS) is a mechanism to gently
constrain textual requirements. The EARS patterns provide structured guidance
that enable authors to write high quality textual requirements. [Mavin]
#+end_quote

The 2009 paper sets this against other notations: they buy precision at the
price of translating from the source text and training their readers.

#+begin_quote
Some experts advocate the use of other notations to in-crease precision and
minimise problems such as ambiguity. However, use of non-textual notations
requires translation of the source requirements, which can introduce further
errors. There is also a training overhead associated with the introduction of
new notations. A small set of structural rules was developed to address eight
common requirement problems including ambiguity, complexity and vagueness. The
ruleset allows all natural language requirements to be expressed in one of
five simple templates. [RE'09 abstract]
#+end_quote

The eight problems are not named in the abstract; only ambiguity, complexity
and vagueness are. The paper's case study applied the ruleset "whilst
extracting aero engine control system requirements from an airworthiness
regulation document", and reports "qualitative and quantitative improvements
compared with a conventional textual requirements specification" [RE'09
abstract].

Mavin gives the reasons practitioners adopt it:

#+begin_quote
EARS has proved popular with practitioners because it is lightweight, there is
little training overhead, no specialist tool is necessary, and the resultant
requirements are easy to read. [Mavin]
#+end_quote

He also says it "is especially effective for requirements authors who must
write requirements in English, but whose first language is not English"
[Mavin]. The originators, ten years on, put its value in its simplicity:

#+begin_quote
As the original authors, we were initially unsure about publishing because we
thought that EARS was too simple: almost too obvious to be of value. It turns
out that it is this simplicity that is perhaps the greatest strength of EARS.
[Ten Years of EARS, abstract]
#+end_quote

* It came from extracting requirements from regulations

#+begin_quote
Mav and colleagues at Rolls-Royce PLC developed EARS whilst analysing the
airworthiness regulations for a jet engine's control system. The regulations
contained high level objectives, a mixture of implicit and explicit
requirements at different levels, lists, guidelines and supporting
information. [Mavin]
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
In the process of extracting and simplifying the requirements, Mav noticed
that the requirements all followed a similar structure. He found that
requirements were easiest to read when the clauses always appeared in the
same order. [Mavin]
#+end_quote

The notation was found in existing requirements, not designed and then
imposed. It is used at Airbus, Bosch, Dyson, Honeywell, Intel, NASA,
Rolls-Royce and Siemens, and taught at universities in six countries [Mavin].

* One sentence, its clauses always in the same order

#+begin_quote
There is a set syntax (structure), with an underlying ruleset. A small number
of keywords are used to denote the different clauses of an EARS requirement.
The clauses are always in the same order, following temporal logic. [Mavin]
#+end_quote

The generic form:

#+begin_example
While <optional pre-condition>, when <optional trigger>, the <system name> shall <system response>
#+end_example

#+begin_quote
The EARS ruleset states that a requirement must have: Zero or many
preconditions; Zero or one trigger; One system name; One or many system
responses. [Mavin]
#+end_quote

- *One trigger at most.* A requirement with two triggers is two
  requirements.
- *One system name.* The sentence names the system responsible; it is the
  subject of "shall".
- *"Temporal logic"* means that the state comes before the trigger, and both
  come before the response.

* Five patterns, named by the clauses they use

Each is quoted from [Mavin], definition, template and example.

| Pattern            | Keyword     | Template                                                        |
|--------------------+-------------+-----------------------------------------------------------------|
| Ubiquitous         | none        | The <system name> shall <system response>                       |
| State driven       | While       | While <precondition(s)>, the <system name> shall <system response> |
| Event driven       | When        | When <trigger>, the <system name> shall <system response>       |
| Optional feature   | Where       | Where <feature is included>, the <system name> shall <system response> |
| Unwanted behaviour | If ... then | If <trigger>, then the <system name> shall <system response>    |

- *Ubiquitous*: "always active (so there is no EARS keyword)". Example: "The
  mobile phone shall have a mass of less than XX grams."
- *State driven*: "active as long as the specified state remains true".
  Example: "While there is no card in the ATM, the ATM shall display “insert
  card to begin”."
- *Event driven*: "specify how a system must respond when a triggering event
  occurs". Example: "When “mute” is selected, the laptop shall suppress all
  audio output."
- *Optional feature*: "apply in products or systems that include the
  specified feature". Example: "Where the car has a sunroof, the car shall
  have a sunroof control panel on the driver door."
- *Unwanted behaviour*: "used to specify the required system response to
  undesired situations". Example: "If an invalid credit card number is
  entered, then the website shall display “please re-enter credit card
  details”."

The ATM and website examples give the exact text the system shows, in quotation
marks, as the system response.

* Complex requirements combine the keywords

#+begin_quote
The simple building blocks of the EARS patterns described above can be
combined to specify requirements for richer system behaviour. Requirements
that include more than one EARS keyword are called Complex requirements.
[Mavin]
#+end_quote

#+begin_example
While <precondition(s)>, When <trigger>, the <system name> shall <system response>
#+end_example

Example: "While the aircraft is on ground, when reverse thrust is commanded,
the engine control system shall enable reverse thrust." "Complex requirements
for unwanted behaviour also include the If-Then keywords." [Mavin]

* Where EARS stops (secondary)

No primary source read here states the limits. Wikipedia reports that
requirements with more than three preconditions make unwieldy sentences;
that some requirements are better written as mathematical formulae, decision
tables or state-transition diagrams; and that EARS suits poorly the
non-functional requirements that cannot be framed as conditional behaviour,
such as architectural constraints [Wikipedia, citing QRA Corp., "When Not to
Use EARS", 2025]. QRA's article was not read.

* Kiro uses the event-driven pattern for its requirements

Amazon's Kiro, an agentic IDE, writes a feature's requirements in EARS:

#+begin_quote
The requirements.md file uses EARS (Easy Approach to Requirements Syntax)
notation to provide structured, testable requirements [Kiro]
#+end_quote

Its documented pattern is "WHEN [condition/event] THE SYSTEM SHALL [expected
behavior]", with the example "WHEN a user submits a form with invalid data THE
SYSTEM SHALL display validation errors next to the relevant fields" [Kiro].
Its documentation claims that "Each requirement can be directly translated
into test cases" [Kiro]. It shows only the event-driven pattern, and drops
the comma Mavin places after the trigger.

* Worked example (own illustration)

Not quoted; written for this entry. It restates the library renewal from the
Cockburn reference (Use Case 12: Renew a borrowed book) so the two forms can
be compared.

#+begin_example
When a Borrower asks to renew a loan, the LendingLibrary system shall set the
loan's due date one loan period after the renewal date.

When a renewal is recorded, the LendingLibrary system shall show the Borrower
the new due date.

If another borrower has reserved the book, then the LendingLibrary system
shall refuse the renewal and show "This book is reserved; your due date
stands".

If the loan has reached the renewal limit, then the LendingLibrary system
shall refuse the renewal and show "This loan cannot be renewed again".

While a loan is overdue beyond the grace period, the LendingLibrary system
shall refuse any renewal of it.

Where the library sets no renewal limit, the LendingLibrary system shall
accept any number of renewals of a loan.
#+end_example

Points the example is built to show: every sentence names the system as the
subject of "shall"; each has at most one trigger; the refusals use the
unwanted-behaviour pattern and give the exact text shown; the overdue rule is
a state, not an event, so it uses While; the library's configuration uses
Where. Nothing here says who the Borrower is, what they were trying to
achieve, or in what order the sentences happen; those are what the use case
holds and EARS does not.
