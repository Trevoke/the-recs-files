#+TITLE: Evans's Ubiquitous Language — Reference
#+STARTUP: overview

Not a project document. This records what the source material actually says, so
that when the project borrows a form or a term we can check the borrowing.
Paraphrased from the sources listed at the end, with the load-bearing sentences quoted.

*What it establishes.* Eric Evans named the Ubiquitous Language in
/Domain-Driven Design/ (2003), chapter 2: one language, built on the domain
model, that developers and domain experts use alike in speech, writing,
diagrams and code, so that nobody has to translate. A change in that language
is a change in the model, and is carried into the code by renaming. The
language holds only inside one bounded context; across contexts the same word
may mean different things, and a context map records how they translate.
Evans treats documents and diagrams as supports for the language rather than
as its home: the code and the spoken language carry the model, and a document
that stops shaping either is left behind. This entry gives what Evans wrote,
what Fowler and Vernon added, what practitioners report going wrong, and —
marked as analysis — how a register of terms with the rules listed in the
last section compares.

A note on the quotations. Chapter 2 is quoted from Evans's /final manuscript/
(dated 15 April 2003), whose wording may differ slightly from the printed book;
page numbers given are where the printed book's section starts, taken from its table of contents
and index. The /Domain-Driven Design Reference/ (2015) is quoted from the PDF
Evans publishes, and its page numbers are that PDF's.

* The ubiquitous language is a language structured around the domain model
The Reference's glossary defines it in one sentence (p. vi):

#+begin_quote
ubiquitous language: A language structured around the domain model and used
by all team members within a bounded context to connect all the activities of
the team with the software.
#+end_quote

Evans's three-point summary of DDD puts it third: "Speak a ubiquitous language
within an explicitly bounded context" (Reference, p. 1). The book frames the
language as the model seen from another side (ch. 2, printed pp. 24–27):

#+begin_quote
A model can be viewed as a language. The vocabulary includes the names of
classes and prominent operations. The language provides the means to discuss
rules that have been made explicit in the model.
#+end_quote

The vocabulary is therefore not only nouns. It includes operations and rules,
and Evans adds that it is "supplemented with terms from high-level organizing
principles imposed on the model" (context maps, large-scale structure) and
"enriched with the names of patterns the team commonly applies to the domain
model". Fowler's summary (2006) adds why it must be rigorous: the language is
"based on the Domain Model used in the software - hence the need for it to be
rigorous, since software doesn't cope well with ambiguity."

* The problem it solves is the cost of translation
Without a shared language, Evans says, every conversation crosses a divide,
and someone translates (ch. 2, "Ubiquitous Language", printed p. 24):

#+begin_quote
On a project without a common language, developers have to translate for
domain experts. Domain experts translate between developers and still other
domain experts. Translation is always inaccurate and hides disconnects in
understanding between the domain experts and developers, between different
developers and between different domain experts. Translation muddles model
concepts, which leads to destructive refactoring of code.
#+end_quote

He names two further costs. The few people who become bilingual "become
bottlenecks of information flow, and their translations are inexact". And
"the indirectness of communication conceals the formation of schisms, where
different team members use terms differently but don't realize it". The
pattern's problem statement, as the Reference gives it (p. 3):

#+begin_quote
Translation blunts communication and makes knowledge crunching anemic.

Yet none of these dialects can be a common language because none serves all
needs.
#+end_quote

The answer is not the domain experts' jargon adopted wholesale, nor the
developers' design vocabulary: it is a third thing built from the model, "a
common language that is more than the lowest common denominator".

* The language is used everywhere: code, speech, diagrams and writing
The pattern's resolution, as printed in the Reference (p. 4):

#+begin_quote
Use the model as the backbone of a language. Commit the team to exercising
that language relentlessly in all communication within the team and in the
code. Within a bounded context, use the same language in diagrams, writing,
and especially speech.
#+end_quote

Speech is singled out. The section "Modeling Out Loud" (printed p. 30) argues
that people have "a genius for spoken language", that "rough edges are easy to
hear", and that a team should "describe scenarios out loud using the elements
and interactions of the model, combining concepts in ways allowed by the
model. Find easier ways to say what you need to say and then take those new
ideas back down to the diagrams and code." The domain experts are bound by it
too: they will speak outside it to give context, "but within its scope, they
should use it, and raise concerns when they find it awkward or incomplete… or
wrong."

* A change in the language is a change in the model
The 2015 Reference states this as a sentence of its own in the pattern's
resolution (p. 4):

#+begin_quote
Recognize that a change in the language is a change to the model.
#+end_quote

The book carries the same claim in the body of the section rather than in the
resolution (printed pp. 24–27):

#+begin_quote
Persistent use of the language will force its weaknesses into the open. The
team will experiment and find alternatives to awkward terms or structure. As
gaps are found, new words will enter the discussion. With a commitment to the
model-based language, these changes will be recognized as changes in the
domain model and will lead to updates of class diagrams and renaming of
classes and methods in the code.
#+end_quote

The converse is in "Model-Driven Design" (Reference, p. 6): "The code becomes
an expression of the model, so a change to the code may be a change to the
model. Its effect must ripple through the rest of the project's activities
accordingly." "Hands-on Modelers" (p. 7) warns that developers who do not
realise "that changing code changes the model" will weaken it by refactoring.

* The language evolves by experiment, and the code is refactored to follow
Evans gives the sequence in the resolution (Reference, p. 4):

#+begin_quote
Iron out difficulties by experimenting with alternative expressions, which
reflect alternative models. Then refactor the code, renaming classes,
methods, and modules to conform to the new model. Resolve confusion over terms
in conversation, in just the way we come to agree on the meaning of ordinary
words.
#+end_quote

Each side has a duty. "Domain experts should object to terms or structures
that are awkward or inadequate to convey domain understanding; developers
should watch for ambiguity or inconsistency that will trip up design" (p. 3).
Agreement is reached the way ordinary words are agreed — by use — and not by a
declared approval step; Evans names no one who ratifies a term. Old material
must follow: "As the new language evolves, the domain experts must make the
extra effort to adopt it, and retrofit any old documents that are still
important" (ch. 2, "One Team, One Language", printed p. 32). The stopping
condition is fluency (same section):

#+begin_quote
By using it pervasively and not being satisfied until it flows, we approach a
model that is complete and comprehensible, made up of simple elements that
combine to express complex ideas.
#+end_quote

Fowler stresses the same evolution: the language and model "should evolve as
the team's understanding of the domain grows" (paraphrased; his wording was
not reproduced verbatim by the fetch).

* One team speaks one language, and technical vocabulary is an extension of it
"One Team, One Language" (printed p. 32) rejects keeping a separate language
for business experts ("Too abstract for them" — "Forget them") and says "If
sophisticated domain experts don't understand the model, there is something
wrong with the model." The model's terms are the experts' jargon "cleaned up",
with "sharper, narrower, definitions", which experts should learn and "object
if these definitions diverge from the meanings in the field".

Evans does not forbid jargon on either side; he fixes its status (section from printed
p. 32):

#+begin_quote
Multiplicity of language is often necessary, but the division should never be
between the domain experts and the developers.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
But these are extensions to the language. These dialects should not contain
alternative vocabularies for the same domain that reflect distinct models.
#+end_quote

Figure 2.3, "Ubiquitous Language is Cultivated in the Intersection of
Jargons", places domain model terms, the names of bounded contexts, the
terminology of large-scale structure and many pattern names in the language,
and puts business terms developers don't understand, business terms that
don't appear in the design, and purely technical aspects of design outside it.
(The figure's exact layout could not be checked from the extracted text; the
labels are certain, the regions they sit in are inferred from the caption.)

* The language holds only inside a bounded context
"A UBIQUITOUS LANGUAGE based on the domain model assumes there is just one
model in play" (ch. 2, end of the "Ubiquitous Language" section). Where several models exist, each has a
boundary (Reference, p. 2):

#+begin_quote
Model expressions, like any other phrase, only have meaning in context.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
Explicitly define the context within which a model applies. [...] Apply
Continuous Integration to keep model concepts and terms strictly consistent
within these bounds, but don't be distracted or confused by issues outside.
#+end_quote

Inside the boundary, consistency is kept actively: "Relentlessly exercise the
ubiquitous language to hammer out a shared view of the model as the concepts
evolve in different people's heads" (Continuous Integration, p. 5). Fowler's
"BoundedContext" (2014) gives the standard illustration: in an electricity
utility, "meter" meant different things to different departments, and "time
and time again I see this confusion recur with polysemes like 'Customer' and
'Product'." Vernon is categorical: "There is one Ubiquitous Language per
Bounded Context", and "If you try to apply a single Ubiquitous Language to an
entire enterprise, or worse, universally among many enterprises, you will
fail."

* A context map names each context and the translation between them
Across boundaries, Evans keeps the language honest by naming the boundaries
themselves (Reference, "Context Map", p. 29):

#+begin_quote
Identify each model in play on the project and define its bounded context.
[...] Name each bounded context, and make the names part of the ubiquitous
language.

Describe the points of contact between the models, outlining explicit
translation for any communication, highlighting any sharing, isolation
mechanisms, and levels of influence.
#+end_quote

Translation, which the language eliminates inside a context, is thus kept and
made explicit between contexts. "Map the existing terrain. Take up
transformations later."

* Two words for one concept and one word for two concepts are the signs of a split model
Chapter 14, "Recognizing Splinters Within a Bounded Context", says "the early
warning sign is usually a confusion of language", and names two forms:

#+begin_quote
Combining elements of distinct models causes two categories of problems:
duplicate concepts and false cognates.
#+end_quote

Duplicate concepts are "two model elements (and attendant implementations)
that actually represent the same concept", which drift apart because the
reanalysis that should follow each change "doesn't happen in reality". False
cognates are "when two people who are using the same term (or implemented
object) think they are talking about the same thing, but really are not" —
"slightly less common, but more insidiously harmful". Evans does not state a
rule "no synonyms" in those words; the rule follows from duplicate concepts
and from "these dialects should not contain alternative vocabularies". His
chapter 1 example credits a good model with having "consolidated many synonyms
and slight variations in descriptions", and his recipe for merging two
contexts includes "the hard work of identifying synonyms and mapping any
terms that are not already being translated".

* The model is not the diagram, and the code carries the detail
"Documents and Diagrams" (printed p. 35) holds that UML conveys relationships
but not "two of the most important aspects of a model: the meaning of
concepts it represents, and what they are meant to do". Diagrams serve best
when minimal. Then (same section):

#+begin_quote
The vital detail about the design is captured in the code. A well-written
implementation should transparently reveal the model underlying it.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
Always remember that the model is not the diagram. The diagram's purpose is to
help communicate and explain the model.
#+end_quote

Evans prefers "a text document illustrated with selective and simplified
diagrams" to a diagram annotated with text.

* A document earns its place by shaping the language, or it is left behind
"Written Design Documents" (printed p. 37) opens on decay: "Once a document
takes on a persistent form, it often loses its connection with the flow of
the project. It is left behind by the evolution of the code, or it is left
behind by the evolution of the language of the project." Evans gives two
tests. First, "a document shouldn't try to do what the code already does
well"; documents should "illuminate meaning, [...] give insight into
large-scale structures, and [...] focus attention on core elements". Second
(same section):

#+begin_quote
The second criterion is that a document must be involved in project
activities. The easiest way to judge this is to observe its interaction with
the UBIQUITOUS LANGUAGE. Is the document written in the language people speak
on the project (now)? Is it written in the language embedded in the code?
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
If the terms explained in a design document don't start showing up in
conversations and code, it is not fulfilling its purpose.
#+end_quote

A document the language has moved past "could actually create confusion and
hurt the project", and keeping it current "through sheer will and discipline
wastes effort, if the document isn't playing an important role". Code is not
a safe harbour either ("Executable Bedrock", printed p. 40): "a method name can
be ambiguous, misleading, or out of date with the internals of the method."

Evans also allows /explanatory models/ (printed p. 41) — pictures or models
used only to teach the domain — on condition that "everyone must be conscious
of the distinction" from the model that drives the design.

* Vernon treats a glossary as a starting point, not the language
Vernon (/Implementing Domain-Driven Design/, ch. 1, section "How to Do DDD")
defines the language as "a shared language developed by the team—a team
comprised of both domain experts and software developers", and says it is
"not just a bunch of business jargon being forced on developers". Among the
first steps he lists: "Create a glossary of terms with simple definitions.
List alternative terms including the ones that show promise and the ones that
didn't work, and why." He then says such artifacts are "only the genesis to
the development of the Ubiquitous Language that will very soon be expressed
in your system's source code", and:

#+begin_quote
That's why in the end it is team speech and the model in the code that is the
most enduring and the only guaranteed current denotation of the Ubiquitous
Language.
#+end_quote

* Practitioners report the same handful of failures
- /Fractured language/ — expert jargon, developer jargon and code names drift
  apart; Evans's own problem statement (above).
- /Glossary and document rot/ — a written glossary falls behind speech and
  code. Evans (documents "left behind") and Vernon (speech and code are "the
  only guaranteed current denotation") both predict it. Jovanović (2026)
  prescribes the remedy many teams use: "Keep the agreed terms in a glossary
  next to the code [...] When a term changes or a new one appears, update the
  glossary in the same pull request that renames the code."
- /Synonyms/ — Ducin (2024): "Don't introduce synonyms. In Ubiquitous
  Language, if two different phrases are used - it has to mean two
  semantically different concepts." Evans's "duplicate concepts" is the
  structural form of the same failure.
- /False cognates and polysemes/ — one word, two meanings, unnoticed (Evans
  ch. 14; Fowler's "meter", "Customer", "Product").
- /Technical vocabulary displacing the domain's/ — CRUD verbs where the domain
  has its own ("Create order" for "Place order"; Jovanović), or developers
  shielding experts from the model, which Evans rejects outright.
- /Vague expert words/ — "The order is done": paid, shipped, delivered?
  (Jovanović). Evans's "cleaned up", "sharper, narrower" definitions answer it.
- /One language for the whole enterprise/ — Vernon: "you will fail".
- /Unmanaged change/ — Esposito (2024) treats the gap as debt and change as a
  business decision: "a gap between business language and code is, strictly
  speaking, a form of technical debt", and "Any change to the language is a
  business-level decision."

* Analysis, not source material: the register's rules against Evans
This section is the entry's own reading, not a report of the sources. It
compares the four register rules given when this entry was commissioned.

- /One word per concept, no synonyms./ Follows Evans in substance: duplicate
  concepts, "alternative vocabularies for the same domain", and his praise of
  a model that consolidated synonyms. The flat rule is practitioners' wording
  (Ducin) more than Evans's. Evans scopes it to one bounded context; a
  register that notes another vocabulary's word beside its own is doing what
  a context map does — explicit translation at a boundary — and is faithful
  so long as the noted word is never used inside.
- /No new term written anywhere before the user agrees to it./ The project's
  own addition. Evans wants terms tried freely out loud and argued over by
  experts and developers, with agreement reached by use, and names no
  approver. The rule is compatible with that if it governs /writing/ rather
  than /speaking/: proposing candidates in conversation is Evans's
  "experimenting with alternative expressions"; the gate on writing is the
  project's way of making "domain experts should object" happen before the
  word spreads. Esposito's "a business-level decision" is the nearest source.
- /A renamed or redefined term is swept through records, code, tests and
  comments in one pass./ Follows Evans: "refactor the code, renaming classes,
  methods, and modules", "retrofit any old documents that are still
  important", and "a change in the language is a change to the model". The
  single pass, and naming comments and tests explicitly, are the project's
  sharpening; Jovanović's same-pull-request rule is the closest practice.
- /A word that survives a change of implementation is language; one that
  would retire with it belongs in an architectural decision./ The project's
  own test. Evans draws a related line — technical jargon is an "extension",
  not the language — but his language is broader than this register: it
  includes "prominent operations", "rules", and pattern names applied to the
  model, so implementation-flavoured words can be ubiquitous in Evans's sense.
  The test is a stricter, project-chosen boundary.
- /What each word means, nothing about behaviour./ Narrower than Evans, whose
  language "provides the means to discuss rules". Faithful if the register is
  read as the glossary of the language, with behaviour carried by the
  project's other records; the language in Evans's sense is all of them
  together, plus the code and speech.
- /The register as authority./ Evans and Vernon both rank a written glossary
  below code and speech as a carrier of the model, and predict it will rot.
  A register declared authoritative departs from that; the sweep rule is what
  keeps the departure safe, by making Evans's "involved in project
  activities" test pass by construction.
