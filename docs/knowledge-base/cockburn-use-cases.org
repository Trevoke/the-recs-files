#+TITLE: Cockburn Use Cases — Reference
#+STARTUP: overview

Not a project document. This records what the source material actually says, so
that when the project borrows a form or a term we can check the borrowing.
Paraphrased from the sources listed at the end, with the load-bearing sentences quoted.

*What it establishes.* Alistair Cockburn's use case is a text form: a contract
for the behaviour of one system under discussion, organised around the goal of
one primary actor, written as a main success scenario plus extensions keyed to
its steps. Every use case is labelled with a design scope and a goal level;
the levels are summary (cloud / kite), user goal (sea level) and subfunction
(fish / clam). Cockburn publishes two templates, casual and fully dressed, and
says which to use is a project standard rather than a matter of right and
wrong.

*On the sources.* The published book (Addison-Wesley, 2001) was not available
to read. Its text is quoted here from two of Cockburn's own pre-publication
drafts that circulate online: draft #3 of 2000-02-21 (abridged, but with the
published book's chapter structure), cited as [d3 p.N], and a fuller 1999
draft, cited as [d99 p.N]. Page numbers are the drafts', not the book's.
Where the two drafts differ, draft #3 is the later word and is preferred.
Cockburn's site, alistair.cockburn.us, returned 404 for the "Basic Use Case
Template" and "Structuring Use Cases with Goals" pages; both documents were
read from mirrors instead (see Sources).

* A use case is a contract for the behaviour of the system under discussion

The use case states how one system behaves when one stakeholder, the primary
actor, asks it for something, and how it protects every other stakeholder
while doing so. Its scenarios are the different ways that request can unfold.

#+begin_quote
A use case captures a contract between the stakeholders of a system about its
behavior. The use case describes the system's behavior under various
conditions as it responds to a request from one of the stakeholders, called
the primary actor. The primary actor initiates an interaction with the system
to accomplish some goal. The system responds, protecting the interests of all
the stakeholders. [d3 p.15, §1.1]
#+end_quote

The four summary definitions that open Part 1 of the book:

#+begin_quote
An actor is anyone or anything with behavior.
A stakeholder is someone or something with a vested interest in the behavior
of the system under discussion (SuD).
The primary actor is the stakeholder who or which initiates an interaction
with the SuD to achieve a goal.
The use case is a contract for the behavior of the SuD. [d3 p.33]
#+end_quote

The earlier, goal-only definition from the 1995/1997 paper remains the core:

#+begin_quote
A use case is a collection of possible scenarios between the system under
discussion and external actors, characterized by the goal the primary actor
has toward the system's declared responsibilities, showing how the primary
actor's goal might be delivered or might fail. [Structuring Use Cases with Goals]
#+end_quote

- *Every use case has two exits.* Some scenarios end in success, some in goal
  failure; the "striped trousers" image has a success leg and a failure leg
  [d3 p.38–39]. A use case "starts at the triggering event, and continues
  until the goal is delivered or abandoned" [d3 p.38].
- *The system under discussion is itself an actor*, named in the scope field
  and referred to by name or as "the system" in the steps [d3 p.54, §4.4].
- *A supporting actor* (formerly "secondary") provides a service to the SuD;
  Cockburn changed the term because "people found the term confusing"
  [d3 p.54, §4.3].

* Stakeholders and interests decide what a use case must contain

The Stakeholders & Interests model, new in the book, extends the Actors &
Goals model. Off-stage stakeholders (owners, regulators) never act in the
steps, but the system acts on their behalf, and that is what puts
validations, logging and state changes into the text.

#+begin_quote
Every sentence in a use case is there because it describes an action that
protects or furthers some interest of some stakeholder. [d3 p.34, ch.2]
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
The use case, as the contract for behavior, captures all and only the
behaviors related to satisfy the stakeholders' interests. [d3 p.40, §2.2]
#+end_quote

Every action is one of three sorts: an interaction between two actors, a
validation to protect a stakeholder, or an internal state change on behalf of
a stakeholder [d3 p.41]. The procedural change is small: list the
stakeholders and their interests first, then use the list to check the body
[d3 p.41]. Cockburn's anecdote: a team that listed its stakeholders' interests
after delivery found its first year of change requests in that list
[d3 p.53–54; d99 p.124].

* Design scope names the system under discussion, and is drawn as an icon

Scope is "the extent of what we consider to be designed by us" [d3 p.44].
Functional scope (which services) is managed by the actor-goal list; design
scope (which system) must be labelled on every use case, because readers
cannot infer it:

#+begin_quote
The only answer I have found is to label each and every use case with its
design scope, using specific names for the most significant design scopes.
[d3 p.49, §3.2]
#+end_quote

Three named scopes, each written with the real name in the field:
enterprise (the organisation, e.g. "MyInsCo"), system (the hardware/software
being built), subsystem (a piece of it opened up) [d3 p.49]. A *black-box*
use case does not mention the insides of the SuD; a *white-box* use case does
[d3 p.49]. Requirements are written black-box; business-process designs are
often white-box [d3 p.20].

Scope icons, placed to the left of the title [d3 p.1 "Icons", p.49–50]:

| Icon                  | Meaning                     |
|-----------------------+-----------------------------|
| building, grey        | organisation, black-box     |
| building, white       | organisation, white-box     |
| box, grey             | system, black-box           |
| box, white            | system, white-box           |
| bolt (threaded screw) | component / subsystem       |

Cockburn adds the *outermost use case*: for each use case, widen the scope
until widening further would bring the primary actor inside, and write a
summary use case at that scope; there are typically only 2–5 of these
[d3 p.50, §3.3].

The 1999 draft used other icons (house; "person at a computer"; "donut" for
components) [d99 p.52]. Draft #3 replaced them; the table above is the later
set.

* Goal levels are summary, user goal and subfunction, named by altitude

Goals nest without limit ("fleas on the fleas", [d3 p.35]), so a writer faces
"What level of goal shall I describe?" at every sentence [d3 p.56]. Cockburn
names three levels, with an altitude and a colour for each [d3 ch.5]:

| Level       | Colour        | Altitude icon       | Meaning                                          |
|-------------+---------------+---------------------+--------------------------------------------------|
| summary     | white         | cloud (very high), kite | involves multiple user goals; hours to years  |
| user goal   | blue          | waves (sea level)   | one person, one sitting, primary actor's goal    |
| subfunction | indigo, black | fish; clam (too low) | needed to carry out user goals                  |

#+begin_quote
In keeping with the altitude nomenclature, I separate five altitudes. You will
only use the middle three, in most situations. [d3 p.60, §5.4]
#+end_quote

- *Cloud* is for "that rarest of occasions, when you see that the steps in the
  use case are themselves white goals"; *kite* "is for most summary use
  cases, whose steps are blue goals" [d3 p.60].
- *Clam*: "Some subfunctions (black) should never be written. Use a clam ...
  to mark a use case that needs to be merged with its calling use case"
  [d3 p.60].
- Goal-level icons go to the top right of the title; as a text alternative,
  append "+" to summary names, "!" or nothing to user-goal names, "-" to
  subfunction names [d3 p.1 "Icons", p.60].
- Summary use cases serve three purposes: they show the context in which user
  goals operate, show life-cycle sequencing of related goals, and give a
  table of contents for lower use cases [d3 p.58, §5.2].
- A subfunction still has a primary actor outside the system; it "follows all
  the rules for use cases" [d3 p.60].
- Terminology: earlier writing used "strategic"; the book chose "summary"
  because it "causes the least confusion" [d3 p.58, footnote]. The 1998
  template calls the middle level "Primary task" [TR96 p.2]; the 1999 draft
  calls it "task" or "user-goal" [d99 p.47–49].

* The user-goal level is found with the coffee-break test

#+begin_quote
A user goal addresses the question, "Can the primary actor go away happy after
having done this?" For a clerk, it would be, "Does your job performance depend
on how many of these you do today?" or the coffee break test: "After I get done
with this, I can take a coffee break." In most circumstances, it passes the
test: One person, one sitting (2-20 minutes). [d3 p.57, §5.1]
#+end_quote

"Log on" fails (logging on 42 times does not satisfy a job responsibility);
"Complete an on-line auction purchase" fails the single-sitting test;
"Register a new customer" and "Buy a book" are likely user goals [d3 p.57].
The 1999 draft phrases the same tests as "Can I go to lunch as soon as this
goal is completed?" and "Can I ask for a raise if I do many of these?"
[d99 p.47].

#+begin_quote
The sea-level / blue / user goals are incredibly important. ... You justify the
existence of the system by the user goals it supports of various primary
actors. [d3 p.58]
#+end_quote

* "Why" moves a goal up a level, "how" moves it down

#+begin_quote
That figure also shows that to find a higher goal level for your goal phrase,
you answer the question "Why is the actor doing this?". [d3 p.60]
#+end_quote

The use case's goal sits one level above its steps' goals; asking "why" of a
step yields the use case's goal, asking "how" of the use case yields its
steps ("Figure: Ask 'why' to shift levels", [d3 p.76; d99 p.127]). The
remedy for a step that barely moves the process forward:

#+begin_quote
Whenever you feel that perhaps the action is not moving sufficiently forward,
ask the question: "Why is this actor doing that?" The answer to your question
is the higher-level goal of that actor, the goal you probably should be writing
in the sentence. [d99 p.58]
#+end_quote

Worked there: "User hits tab key" → why? to reach the address field → why? to
enter name and address → so the step is "User enters name and address"
[d99 p.58]. The reminder card puts it as "Ask 'why' to find a next-higher
level goal" [d3 p.1].

* Casual and fully dressed are two templates, chosen per project

#+begin_quote
I have come to the conclusion that it is incorrect to publish just one use case
template. There must be at least two, a casual one for low-ceremony projects,
and a fully dressed one for higher-ceremony projects. [d3 p.22, §1.2]
#+end_quote

- *Casual*: "written in simple, paragraph, prose style. It is likely to be
  missing project information associated with the use case, and is likely to
  be less rigorous in its description than a fully dressed use case"
  [d3 glossary p.236]. It "short circuits" the template [d3 p.20].
- *Fully dressed*: "written with one of the full templates, identifying
  actors, scope, level, trigger condition, precondition, and all the rest of
  the template header information, plus project annotation information"
  [d3 glossary p.236]; it uses "the full use case template and step numbering
  scheme" [d3 p.20].
- Cockburn's preferred fully dressed style is one column of text, numbered
  steps, no /if/ statements, and the 2a / 2a1 extension numbering [d3 p.68].
- Neither is wrong; "A use case valid on one project is not a valid use case on
  another project" — a project must say which form, which fields are
  mandatory, and how much variation it tolerates [d3 p.24]. Scope and level
  are labelled in both: "The use of symbols are completely optional. Labeling
  the scope and level are not." [d3 p.16].

The fully dressed template (draft #3, "Use Case 24/7", [d3 p.68–69]):

| Field                            | Template text                                                   |
|----------------------------------+-----------------------------------------------------------------|
| name                             | "the goal as a short active verb phrase"                        |
| Context of use                   | "a longer statement of the goal, if needed, its normal occurrence conditions" |
| Scope                            | "design scope, what system is being considered black-box under design" |
| Level                            | "one of: Summary, User-goal, Subfunction"                       |
| Primary Actor                    | "a role name for the primary actor, or description"             |
| Stakeholders & Interests         | "list of stakeholders and key interests in the use case"        |
| Precondition                     | "what we expect is already the state of the world"              |
| Minimal Guarantees               | "how the interests are protected under all exits"               |
| Success Guarantees               | "the state of the world if goal succeeds"                       |
| Trigger                          | "what starts the use case, may be time event"                   |
| Main Success Scenario            | "the steps of the scenario from trigger to goal delivery, and any cleanup after" |
| Extensions                       | "<step altered> <condition>: <action or sub-use case>"           |
| Technology and Data Variations List | "the variations that will cause eventual bifurcation in the scenario" |
| Related Information              | "whatever your project needs for additional information"        |

Older names for the same fields: "Goal in Context", "Success End Condition",
"Failed End Condition", "Sub-Variations" [TR96 p.2]; "Failed End Protection",
"I/O Variations" [d99 p.65]. TR96 also lists optional Priority, Performance
Target, Frequency, Superordinate/Subordinate Use Cases, Channels, Secondary
Actors, Open Issues and Schedule, and advises filling the template in several
passes rather than at once [TR96 p.2, p.8].

* Preconditions are guaranteed by the system and never re-checked

#+begin_quote
The precondition names something that you want the SuD to ensure has happened
before the use case starts. ... because these are the conditions that will
never be checked during the steps in the use case. [d99 p.132]
#+end_quote

If an extension handles the failure of a precondition, one of the two is a
writing error; if the use case can technically start without it, the
precondition is a writing error [d99 p.132]. Things that are usually but not
necessarily true belong in Context of use, not Precondition [d99 p.55].

- *Minimal guarantee*: "how the interests are protected under all exits"
  [d3 p.68]; typically that the system logged how far it got [d99 p.55–56].
  Example: "sufficient logging information that PAF can detect that something
  went wrong and can ask the user to provide details" [d3 p.17].
- *Success guarantee*: what interests are satisfied at successful
  completion; pass/fail test, "Are all stakeholders interests satisfied?"
  [d3 pass/fail #14].
- *Trigger*: what starts the use case, "may be time event" [d3 p.68]. Three
  kinds: the primary actor initiating, the primary actor using an
  intermediary, or "a time- or state-based initiation" [d3 p.42].

Writing the guarantees first is recommended: the failure protection "often
turns up a missing element in the main success scenario" [d99 p.124, p.133].

* The main success scenario is 3–9 goal-achieving steps, with no UI detail

#+begin_quote
The main success scenario is the one written in full, from trigger to
completion, including goal delivery and any bookkeeping that happens after. It
is a typical and illustrative success scenario, even though it may not be the
only success path. [d3 glossary p.235]
#+end_quote

Step count: "Use 3 to 9 steps to meet all interests and guarantees"
[d3 p.2, Writing Process step 8]; pass/fail test #17 "Does it have 3 - 9
steps?" [d3]. Elsewhere the same draft says "Use 3-10 steps per use case"
[d3 p.61, §5.5]; the 1999 draft said 3–11 [d99 p.12]. The consistent
claim is "not much more than nine".

The step guidelines of chapter 7 (titles from [d3 TOC, §7.2]):

1. It uses simple grammar — "Noun... verb... direct object... prepositional
   phrase." [d99 p.58]
2. It shows clearly "who has the ball" — the actor is the subject, usually the
   first or second word.
3. It is written from a bird's eye point of view, not "inside looking out".
4. It shows the process moving distinctly forward.
5. It shows the actor's intent, not movements ("semantics, not dialog",
   [d99 p.59]).
6. It contains a "reasonable" set of actions (one transaction has four parts:
   request, validate, change state, reply).
7. It doesn't "check whether", it "validates".
8. It optionally mentions the timing.
9. Idiom: "User has System A kick System B".
10. Idiom: "Do steps x-y until condition".

#+begin_quote
There is only one style of sentence used in writing action steps in a use case:
a sentence in the present tense, with an active verb in the active voice,
describing an actor successfully achieving a goal that moves the process
forward. [d99 p.122]
#+end_quote

Numbers are labels for reference, not a claim of strict order: "The numbering
serves only so that we can refer to a step, not because the step occurs in
that sequence" [d99 p.62]. Unnumbered notes between steps ("Steps 3-5 can
happen in any order") are allowed [d99 p.62].

* Extensions are numbered by the step at which the condition is detected

#+begin_quote
The numbers in the extensions refer to the step numbers in the main success
scenario at which each different situation gets detected (for instance, steps
4a and 4b indicate two different conditions that could show up at step 4).
[d3 p.16]
#+end_quote

Numbering conventions [d99 p.74–76]:

- Letter after the step number for each condition at that step: 2a, 2b.
- Colon after the condition, to mark where it ends: "2a. Insufficient funds:".
- Handling steps are indented and numbered under the condition: 2a1, 2a2.
- A condition possible at several steps names the range: "2-5a. User quit
  suddenly:".
- A condition possible at any time uses an asterisk, listed first:
  "*a. Network goes down:".
- Failures within failures continue the scheme (6a1a, 6a1b); at about four
  levels of indentation or three pages, break the fragment out into a sub use
  case [d99 p.76–77].

An extension has two parts, the *condition* and the *handling*, and they are
written in two passes: brainstorm every condition first, then write the
handling, because handling "takes much more energy" [d3 p.30, §1.5].

#+begin_quote
Guideline 11: The condition says what was detected. [d3 TOC, §8.1]
#+end_quote

Conditions are phrases or short past-tense sentences ("Invalid PIN:",
"Network is down:") [d99 p.74], restricted to what the system can detect and
must handle: "Include all that the system can detect and must handle" [d3 p.2].

#+begin_quote
Each will ends back in the MSS, at a separate success exit, or in failure.
[d3 p.2, Writing Process step 10]
#+end_quote

So a handling fragment either repairs the step and rejoins, retries the step,
or abandons the goal ("the use case ends", "Fail!"); "go to step N" is
written only when the return point is not obvious [d99 p.75–76]. Extensions
include alternative *success* paths as well as failures [d99 p.72]. A failed
sub use case rolls up into one condition in its caller: "The outer, or
calling, use case simply builds on the success or failure of the step naming
the sub use case" [d3 p.39].

Extensions are not variations. "Write these into the Technology and Data
Variations List section of the use case, not in the Extensions section"
[d3 p.65, ch.9] — variations record that /how/ differs while /what/ is the
same (pay by cheque, transfer or credit).

* Sub use cases are linked by naming them in a step

"An action step can be a simple step or the name of another use case"; the
called use case's name is underlined or italicised [d3 p.66, §10.1]. This is
UML's /include/. Cockburn treats /extends/ use cases as rare (asynchronous
interrupting services, locked requirements) and advises against /generalizes/
and published extension points [d3 p.66–67, App. A]. Use cases are
"fundamentally a text form"; the diagram is only a table of contents
[d3 p.15, p.202–203].

* A use case's steps break into features, and an XP user story can be one

The book gives this its own section, §17.2 "Use Cases to Task or Feature
Lists", with a worked "Feature list for Capture Trade-in" [d3 TOC, p.167–170].
Draft #3 lists the section but omits its body; the text below is from the
1999 draft's earlier version, "Use cases to detailed requirements (engineering
tasks)" [d99 p.100–101], and from a 2002 article.

The derivation, as a practitioner reported it to Cockburn:

#+begin_quote
we essentially used the name of each use case as a feature set, and each step
within the scenarios as features. [d99 p.100]
#+end_quote

The same correspondent found that "the exact text from the scenarios doesn't
stand on its own very well as a line-item feature", and kept rewording it
[d99 p.101]. Cockburn's reply locates the tension: designers work "on a
single line item buried in one use cases - or maybe, the same line repeated
across multiple use cases", while buyers, users and testers "want to see the
application in full flow, delivering value" [d99 p.101]. The pieces are units
of work:

#+begin_quote
Each piece becomes a program "feature" or "mechanism" or "design task" that
will be assigned, tracked, and checked off. [d99 p.101]
#+end_quote

He would rather not keep the list as a document of its own:

#+begin_quote
When I get my preferences, I prefer to write use cases, and generate the task
list informally, rather than creating and maintaining two official documents,
one usage-centered and one feature-centered. [d99 p.101]
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
Use cases have a basic mismatch with feature lists. The same system feature is
likely to show up as a line item in multiple use cases. ... Anyone who
requires both use cases and feature lists is asking for double work.
[Ten Years Later]
#+end_quote

Cockburn places the XP user story at the same grain as a line of that list:

#+begin_quote
An XP user story, in its brevity, may look either like the use case briefs
described in "A sample of use case briefs" on page 47, or a system feature as
described in "Feature list for Capture Trade-in" on page 170. [d3 p.148, ch.18]
#+end_quote

A story is "a sort of promissory note to have a further discussion about the
requirements around a small piece of functionality", and works where "the
usage and business experts sit right with the developers" [d3 p.148].

* Cockburn names the mistakes he sees most often

#+begin_quote
The most common mistakes in writing are leaving out the subjects of sentences,
making assumptions about the user interface design, and using goal levels that
are too low. [d3 p.149, ch.19]
#+end_quote

Chapter 19 "Mistakes Fixed" lists: no system; no primary actor; too many user
interface details; very low goal levels; purpose and content not aligned
[d3 TOC]. Others named elsewhere in the drafts:

- *Too much precision too early*: "The mistake is getting too caught up in
  precision and rigor, when it is not needed" [d3 p.21]; work breadth-first
  through four precision levels — actors and goals, brief or main success
  scenario, extension conditions, extension handling [d3 p.1, p.30].
- */If/ statements in the scenario* instead of extensions [d99 p.69, p.136].
- *"Checks" instead of "validates"* [d99 p.59].
- *Writing from the system's point of view* ("Get ATM card and PIN") instead
  of the bird's eye view [d99 p.59].
- *Preconditions that the system cannot guarantee* [d99 p.54, p.132].
- *Treating the UML ellipses as the use cases* ("The Great Drawing Hoax",
  [d3 p.202–203]).
- *Writing too many low-level use cases*: one team replaced over a hundred
  pages of indigo use cases with six sea-level ones [d3 p.58].

The 31 pass/fail questions at the back of draft #3 (title, scope, level,
primary actor, preconditions, guarantees, each step, extension conditions,
variations) are the closest thing to a checklist the source offers [d3, final
two pages].

* Worked example, casual form (own illustration)

Not quoted; written for this entry to show the shape. Cockburn's own matched
pair is "Use Case 4: Buy Something (Casual version)" and "Use Case 5: Buy
Something (Fully dressed version)" [d3 p.22–24].

#+begin_example
Use Case: Renew a borrowed book  (system, black-box; user goal / sea level)
Primary actor: Borrower.   Scope: LendingLibrary system.   Level: User goal.

The Borrower identifies themselves and picks the loan to renew. The system
validates that the loan is renewable — not overdue past the grace period, not
reserved by another borrower, under the renewal limit — sets the new due date,
and records the renewal against the loan. The Borrower sees the new due date.

If another borrower has reserved the book, the system refuses the renewal and
tells the Borrower the original due date stands. If the renewal limit is
reached, the system refuses and says so. At any time the Borrower can stop
without changing the loan.
#+end_example

* Worked example, fully dressed form (own illustration)

Not quoted; same use case, laid out on the draft #3 template.

#+begin_example
Use Case 12: Renew a borrowed book
Context of use: A Borrower wants to keep a book past its due date. Usually done
  online in the days before the due date.
Scope: LendingLibrary system (black-box)
Level: User goal
Primary Actor: Borrower
Stakeholders & Interests:
  Borrower - keeps the book longer without a fine.
  Reserving borrower - gets the book when it is due back.
  Library - every loan's due date and renewal count are accurate.
Precondition: The Borrower is identified to the system.
Minimal Guarantees: The loan is unchanged unless the renewal was recorded.
Success Guarantees: The loan has a new due date and an incremented renewal
  count; the Borrower has been shown the new due date.
Trigger: The Borrower asks to renew a loan.

Main Success Scenario:
1. Borrower selects the loan to renew.
2. System validates that the loan is renewable.
3. System sets the new due date and records the renewal.
4. System shows the Borrower the new due date.

Extensions:
*a. Borrower abandons the request:
    *a1. System leaves the loan unchanged; the use case ends.
2a. Another borrower has reserved the book:
    2a1. System tells the Borrower the renewal is refused and the original
         due date stands; the use case ends in failure.
2b. Renewal limit reached:
    2b1. System tells the Borrower the limit and the original due date;
         the use case ends in failure.
2c. Loan is overdue beyond the grace period:
    2c1. System tells the Borrower to Return a borrowed book (UC 9);
         the use case ends in failure.

Technology and Data Variations List:
1. Borrower may renew online, by phone through staff, or at a kiosk.
#+end_example

Points the example is built to show: name is an active verb phrase naming the
primary actor's goal; four steps, each with an actor as subject; step 2
"validates" rather than "checks", so its failures attach to it as 2a–2c; the
step numbers in the extensions are where each condition is detected; "*a"
covers a condition possible at any step; 2c names a sub use case in a step;
the minimal guarantee protects the library's interest on every exit.
